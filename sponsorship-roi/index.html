How to measure sponsorship ROI

The formula, why media value isn't return, and how to grade every number so the result survives the finance meeting. With a worked example in pounds.

Grade your evidence in 30sDownload the sample report

Most sponsorship ROI figures don't survive a finance meeting. They're built on media value: what the logo exposure would have cost as advertising. That's a useful number, but it isn't a return. A return is money the sponsor made because of the deal: leads, sales and customers. This guide shows how to measure that honestly, for any sport, with data most clubs and brands already have.

What sponsorship ROI actually means

Return on investment compares what a partnership brought in with what it cost. For sponsorship, that means:

ROI = (value of outcomes − total cost) ÷ total cost

Total cost is the rights fee plus activation: hospitality, creative, staff time, prizes and anything else spent to make the deal work. Leaving activation out is the most common way ROI gets inflated.

Value of outcomes is the commercial value of what the partnership produced for the sponsor's business: tagged leads multiplied by what a lead is worth, promo-code revenue, new accounts multiplied by their value, and any extra customers compared with a region or period without the sponsorship.

Why media value is not return

Media value equivalency (MVE, sometimes called AVE) asks "what would this exposure have cost to buy?". It tells a sponsor that the logo was seen, which matters. It doesn't tell them whether anyone bought anything. A deal can carry £500K of media value and sell nothing; another can carry very little and fill a sales pipeline.

That's why a finance director's first question about a sponsorship report is "how do you know?". If the answer is impressions, the deal is easy to cut in a cost review. We keep media value in every report, clearly labelled as context, and never add it to ROI.

Grade every number by its evidence

Not all outcomes are equally certain. A promo-code redemption is a fact; "enquiries went up during the season" is a correlation. So every result gets a grade from A to D for how strong its evidence is:

A IncrementalCompared against no sponsorshipNew customers in the club's region against a control region, or this season against the season beforeYes, in full
B AttributedTracked directly to the dealPromo-code redemptions, tracked links and UTMs, CRM leads tagged to the partnershipYes, in full
C AssociatedMoved during the deal, but not trackedRegional enquiries up during the season, sales uplift with no tagHalf credit, base case only

Report ROI as a range, never one number

A single ROI figure hides its assumptions. We always report two cases:

  • Conservative case: grade A and B outcomes only, the things we can track or compare.
  • Base case: A and B, plus half credit for grade C outcomes that moved during the deal without tracking.

Worked example (fictional club and sponsor)

Riverside FC × Northbank, one season. Total cost including activation: £180,000.

OutcomeGradeValue
+60 incremental accounts, club region vs control regionA£36,000
452 promo-code accounts and 1,210 tagged CRM leadsB£319,600
Untracked regional uplift during the seasonC£60,000
16.9M impressions (media value)D£543,000, context only

Conservative ROI = (£36,000 + £319,600 − £180,000) ÷ £180,000 = 98%
Base-case ROI = (£36,000 + £319,600 + £30,000 − £180,000) ÷ £180,000 = 114%

If media value had been counted, the "ROI" would have looked like 300% or more, and it would have been the first number finance threw out.

How to set up a sponsorship so it can be measured

The best time to plan measurement is before the season starts. Five things make the difference between a grade D report and a grade A or B one:

  1. Write the sponsor's business objective down. Leads, footfall, sales in a region, B2B introductions. One line, agreed by both sides.
  2. Give the sponsor its own promo code or tracked link, ideally one per channel (shirt, LED, social, matchday programme), so redemptions can be counted.
  3. Tag leads in the sponsor's CRM. A source field or campaign tag for anything that comes through the partnership.
  4. Agree what one lead or customer is worth, even roughly: average order value, lifetime value, or a figure from finance.
  5. Keep a comparison. A region the sponsorship doesn't reach, or last season's numbers, so you can show what changed because of the deal.

None of this needs new software or personal data. Totals and exports are enough.

Questions we get asked

What is a good ROI for a sports sponsorship?

There is no single benchmark, because sponsorships buy different things. A useful test is whether the conservative case (grade A and B outcomes only) covers the total cost. If it does, the deal is paying for itself on evidence alone.

Should media value be included in sponsorship ROI?

No. Media value estimates what the exposure would have cost as advertising. It shows the logo was seen, not that anything was sold, so we report it beside ROI as context and never inside it.

Can you measure ROI if we didn't track anything?

Usually, yes, but at a lower grade. Most partnerships have some trackable outcome, such as a code, a tagged lead or a sales total. We report what the evidence supports, say how strong it is, and give you a measurement plan so next season reaches grade A or B.

Do you need personal or customer-level data?

No. We work with totals and aggregates only, such as the number of code redemptions or tagged leads. No personal data changes hands.

Which sports does this work for?

Any sport: football, rugby, cricket, motorsport, basketball, hockey, running and more, plus leagues, venues and events. The method is the same for every one.
