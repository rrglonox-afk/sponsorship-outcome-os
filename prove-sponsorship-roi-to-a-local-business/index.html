How to prove sponsorship ROI to a local business

What local sponsors want, three cheap ways to track it, and how to turn it into an honest ROI figure the owner will trust.

Grade your evidence in 30sRead the full ROI method

Local businesses sponsor clubs and events for practical reasons, and at renewal the owner will ask a practical question: was it worth it? You don't need expensive tools to answer. You need to know what they wanted, one or two simple ways to track it, and an honest way of showing the result.

Start with what the business wants

Ask the owner or manager in the first meeting, and write the answer down. Most local sponsors want one or two of these:

What they wantEvidence you can collectHow strong
More customersOrders using a club-only promo code; "how did you hear about us" answersB tracked
Enquiries and quotes (trades, B2B)A tagged web form or QR code; a source field in their enquiry logB tracked
Introductions to other businessesAn introductions log: who you introduced, what quotes and work followedB tracked
Staff and apprenticesApplications that mention the club, or come through a club channelB tracked
More business in the areaCustomers or sales by postcode, this season against last, or near the ground against further awayA or C
Being seen in the townMatchday attendance, programme readership, social reachD context only

The grades come from our sponsorship ROI method: A and B count in full, C gets half credit in an optimistic case, and D is context, never return.

Three cheap ways to track it

  1. A club-only promo code. One code, used only on club channels (or one per channel: shirt, programme, LED boards, social). The sponsor counts redemptions in their till or website.
  2. A tagged link or QR code. On the programme ad, matchday screens and social posts. Free to set up, and the sponsor sees the visits in their analytics.
  3. An introductions log. For B2B sponsors this is often the whole point. Note every introduction the club makes in hospitality or networking, and ask the sponsor each quarter what came of it.

None of this needs personal data. Totals are enough.

Turning it into ROI

ROI = (value of outcomes − total cost) ÷ total cost

Total cost is the fee plus anything else the sponsor spent to make it work: hospitality, prizes, printing, staff time. Value of outcomes is what the tracked results were worth to the business. For a local business, use gross profit rather than takings where you can, because that's how an owner thinks about it.

Worked example (illustrative, fictional business)

A local café pays £3,000 for a season and spends £500 on matchday offers. Total cost £3,500.

  • 310 orders with the club code at an average £14, at 60% gross margin: about £2,600 (grade B)
  • Two corporate catering jobs from hospitality introductions, £1,800 profit (grade B)
  • Footfall up on matchdays against non-matchdays, not tracked: £600 estimated (grade C)

Conservative ROI (A and B only): (£2,600 + £1,800 − £3,500) ÷ £3,500 = 26%.
Base case (plus half of C): (£4,400 + £300 − £3,500) ÷ £3,500 = 34%.

That's a deal the owner can see paid for itself, with every number traceable. It also shows where next season could do better: more code use, more introductions.

If the honest answer is that it didn't pay back, say so, and show what you'd change. A sponsor who trusts your numbers is more likely to stay than one who's been oversold.

Show it on one page

Owners don't read decks. Give them one page: what they wanted, what happened (with grades), the ROI range, and what you propose for next season at what price. Then follow it with the full renewal pack if they want the detail.

If you're the business

The same method works from your side. Ask the club for a code and a tagged link before the season starts, keep a note of introductions, and judge the deal on the result, not the logo. If you sponsor at a bigger scale and want an independent view, see our Sponsor Value Audit.

Questions we get asked

How do you measure ROI on a local sponsorship?

Agree what the business wants, track it with a club-only promo code, a tagged link or QR code, or an introductions log, then compare the value of those outcomes with the full cost of the deal.

Should media value or reach count as ROI?

No. Reach shows the sponsor was seen, which is worth reporting as context, but it isn't money the business made.

What if the numbers are small?

Show them anyway, graded honestly. A small, well-evidenced return is more persuasive than a big unexplained one, and it shows where next season can improve.
